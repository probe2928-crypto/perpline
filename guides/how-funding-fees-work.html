The short version

A perpetual futures contract never expires, so exchanges use funding to keep its price close to the spot market. At fixed times, traders on one side of the market pay traders on the other side. When the funding rate is positive, longs pay shorts. When it is negative, shorts pay longs. The exchange sets the rate, but the money moves between traders.

The payment is the value of your position multiplied by the funding rate at that moment. It is charged only if you hold a position when funding is settled, and it is completely separate from the trading fee you pay to open and close. That last point matters for rebates: a fee rebate is a share of your trading fees, so it never covers funding.

This guide walks through the calculation in dollars, shows how funding compares with trading fees over different holding periods, and explains what to check on the contract page before you open a position. Where we need a real exchange, we use Bitget, whose own fee FAQ we checked on 3 November 2026.

Why perpetual futures need funding

A quarterly futures contract has an end date. As that date gets closer, its price is pulled toward the spot price, because on expiry the two have to meet. A perpetual has no end date, so nothing pulls it back by itself. If more traders want to be long than short, the perpetual can drift above the spot price and stay there.

Funding fixes that with a small, regular payment. If the perpetual trades above spot, the rate turns positive and longs pay shorts. Holding a long becomes slightly more expensive and holding a short slightly more rewarding, which nudges the two prices back together. When the perpetual trades below spot, the rate turns negative and the payment runs the other way.

Because the rate follows the market, it changes from one funding time to the next. A long that receives funding this week can be paying it next week.

How the funding payment is calculated

Bitget's fee breakdown gives the formula as position value × funding rate. Bitget's trading fees FAQ says funding is exchanged between long and short holders every 8 hours, which works out to three funding times a day on contracts with that interval. The contract page shows a countdown to the next one, and that countdown is the number to trust for the specific market you trade.

Like the trading fee, funding is worked out on the full position value, not the margin. Post $1,000 of margin at 10x and you hold a $10,000 position, so funding is calculated on $10,000. Leverage does not change the rate, but it multiplies what the rate costs you relative to the money you actually put in.

Take that $10,000 long and a funding rate of +0.01%. At the funding time you pay $10,000 × 0.01% = $1, and that amount goes to traders holding shorts. If the rate had been −0.01%, you would have received $1 instead. If you close the position before the funding time, nothing is charged or paid for that interval.

Sources: Understanding Bitget's Fees and the Bitget Trading Fees FAQ, checked 3 Nov 2026. Bitget may use a different interval on some contracts; the countdown on each contract page shows the live schedule.

A single funding payment looks tiny. The cost comes from repetition. The table below shows a $10,000 position held through a run of funding times with an 8-hour interval, at two hypothetical rates. For comparison, opening and closing that same $10,000 position with market orders at Bitget's standard 0.06% taker rate costs $12 in trading fees. A trader who is in and out within a few hours mostly pays trading fees. A trader who holds for a week at a steady +0.01% pays $21 in funding on top, which is already more than the round-trip fee. The longer you hold, the more funding decides your cost.

Funding is not a trading fee, and no rebate covers it

This is the point most often misunderstood. A trading fee is charged by the exchange each time an order fills, and the exchange keeps it. Funding is paid from one group of traders to another. The exchange does not earn it, so there is no referral commission on it, and nothing for a rebate service to share.

What a rebate does cover is the trading fee, whichever side of the market you are on. Through Perpline, 40% of the Bitget futures fees you pay comes back in USDT every Monday. What to check before you open a position

  • The current funding rate and its sign. It is shown on the contract page next to the price. Positive means longs pay; negative means shorts pay.
  • The countdown to the next funding time. Opening a long a few minutes before a large positive funding time means paying it almost at once.
  • How long you plan to hold. For a trade of a few hours, funding is a small line. For a position held for weeks, it can become the biggest cost on the bill.
  • Unusually large rates. A rate many times higher than normal usually means the market is crowded on one side. That is a sign of risk, not a reason to trade.

Receiving funding is not free money. A trader who opens a short only to collect positive funding still carries the full price risk of that short, and one bad move can cost far more than months of funding.

Questions

How are funding fees calculated in crypto futures?

The payment is your position value multiplied by the funding rate at the funding time, as Bitget's fee breakdown puts it. It is calculated on the full position, leverage included, not on your margin. You pay or receive it only if you hold a position when funding is settled.

Who pays funding, longs or shorts?

It depends on the sign of the rate. When the funding rate is positive, longs pay shorts. When it is negative, shorts pay longs. The rate changes every interval; Bitget's fee FAQ says funding is exchanged every 8 hours (checked 3 November 2026).

Does a fee rebate cover funding fees?

No. Funding moves between traders and the exchange does not earn it, so there is no commission to share. A rebate covers trading fees only. Through Perpline on Bitget, 40% of your trading fees comes back, which brings the effective taker rate from 0.06% to 0.036%.
